Project summary

Connect learning investment to workforce decisions.

During Intro to Business Analytics at Simon Business School, our team worked through three connected cases about employee development. The first examined learning effectiveness and capacity use, the second explored employee motivation, and the third evaluated whether historical performance and training information could help explain promotion patterns.

The progression brought descriptive analysis, unsupervised learning, and supervised modeling into one business story. Each method answered a different question: where programs were working, how development needs differed, and how much confidence decision-makers should place in a prediction.

Challenge

Turn fragmented signals into useful recommendations.

Enrollment counts alone cannot explain whether employees learn, apply new skills, or need a different development pathway. The analysis had to bring together course participation, pre- and post-training measures, geography, survey responses, and performance information while respecting differences in their units of observation.

Interpretation mattered as much as technique. Differences between delivery formats can reflect participant selection, clusters depend on modeling choices, and promotion predictions can reproduce historical patterns. Recommendations therefore needed to match the strength of the evidence.

The analysis

Three questions, three complementary approaches.

Where is training effective?

The first case combined six source datasets to examine skill gains, utilization, course popularity, regional differences, and virtual versus in-person delivery. Comparing outcomes by intake skill band helped reveal where aggregate averages concealed different learning needs. The analysis supported recommendations about course redesign, delivery choices, and capacity allocation.

What motivates employees to develop?

The second case analyzed 600 survey responses using principal component analysis to summarize correlated motivation measures, followed by K-means clustering and silhouette evaluation. The team interpreted five motivation segments and examined city-level patterns to inform more targeted development programs.

What can promotion modeling tell us?

The third case compared six statistical and machine-learning approaches using performance, manager ratings, job level, tenure, training, and regional information. ROC-AUC, precision-recall behavior, and threshold tradeoffs helped frame the model as evidence for workforce planning and further investigation.

Evidence

Inspect the workflow.

The later public version was built with AI assistance. It generates fictional records and recreates the three analytical stages in Python; it is separate from the original five-person coursework. Its charts and metrics demonstrate the method rather than validate the original findings. Course datasets and instructor materials remain private.

Results

Recommendations grounded in analytical limits.

The original team analysis identified differences in training gains by course and participant group, developed five employee motivation segments, and compared six promotion models. These findings supported recommendations about learning design, delivery, and talent planning.

The academic promotion report recorded a best ROC-AUC of 0.661 for logistic regression, indicating modest discrimination. That result called for caution in interpreting individual predictions. It represents the original coursework evaluation, not performance of the synthetic reconstruction or a deployed employment system.

The public implementation improves the evaluation structure by choosing the classifier and operating threshold on validation data, then evaluating the selected approach on a separate test set. Its metrics are documented in the repository and depend on the generated data.

Methods

From data preparation to decision context.

The workflow uses pandas and NumPy for preparation, scikit-learn for standardization, PCA, K-means, and model comparison, and Matplotlib and seaborn for visual interpretation. The model comparison includes logistic regression, gradient boosting, random forest, decision tree, a neural network, and a linear-probability benchmark.

Training and testing boundaries, clear feature definitions, and explicit assumptions make the analysis easier to inspect. Public code and generated outputs are accompanied by setup instructions, automated checks, and an explanation of the dataset's limits.

Reflection

Match the recommendation to the evidence.

The central lesson was that analytical sophistication does not automatically create a useful business recommendation. Course averages need context, segments need interpretation, and a predictive score needs an understanding of its errors and the decisions it might influence.

Further work would examine cluster stability, uncertainty in learning comparisons, calibration, missingness, and subgroup performance. A prospective evaluation would be needed to understand whether any recommendation improves outcomes in practice. Human judgment and accountability remain central to decisions affecting employees.
